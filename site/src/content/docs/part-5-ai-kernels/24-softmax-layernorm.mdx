Every transformer layer ends its attention scores with a softmax and wraps
its sublayers in a normalization, usually LayerNorm. Neither does much
arithmetic. Both read a whole row before they can write a single output,
and both break in float32 if you type the textbook formula straight into a
kernel. Feed this row to `exp(x) / sum(exp(x))`:

```python
x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 1000.0], dtype=np.float32)
```

`exp(1000)` does not fit in float32, so it becomes `inf`, the sum becomes
`inf`, and the output is `[0, 0, nan]`. The correct answer is `[0, 0, 1]`.
This chapter fixes that, then cuts the number of times the kernel has to read
the row, which is what decides its speed.

## Softmax and why exp overflows

The softmax turns a row of scores x<sub>1</sub>, ..., x<sub>n</sub> into
probabilities:

y<sub>i</sub> = e<sup>x<sub>i</sub></sup> / (e<sup>x<sub>1</sub></sup> + ... + e<sup>x<sub>n</sub></sup>)

Every y<sub>i</sub> is positive and they add up to 1. The largest score gets
the largest share, which is why attention uses it to weigh values.

The trouble is `exp`. The largest finite float32 is about 3.4 x 10<sup>38</sup>,
so `expf(x)` overflows to infinity for x above ln(3.4 x 10<sup>38</sup>) = {lim32}.
In FP16 the largest finite value is 65504, and `exp(x)` overflows from
x = {lim16}: a score of 12 is enough. Once one `exp` is infinite the sum is infinite and `inf / inf` is NaN. At the other end, a row
of very negative scores makes every `exp` underflow to 0, and `0 / 0` is NaN
too.

### Subtract the max

Multiplying the top and bottom of the fraction by the same number e<sup>-m</sup>
leaves y unchanged:

y<sub>i</sub> = e<sup>x<sub>i</sub> - m</sup> / (e<sup>x<sub>1</sub> - m</sup> + ... + e<sup>x<sub>n</sub> - m</sup>)

Pick m = max x<sub>j</sub>. Then every exponent is at most 0, so every `exp`
is at most 1 and nothing overflows. The largest term is exactly e<sup>0</sup> = 1,
so the sum is at least 1 and nothing divides by zero. Small terms may still
underflow to 0, but only when they are less than about 10<sup>-45</sup> times
the largest, where 0 is the right answer to float32 precision. This is called the
safe softmax; the Triton tutorial's kernel and every kernel in this chapter
compute it.

## Three passes, two passes, one

Written for one row, the safe softmax is three loops over memory:

1. Read the row to find the max m.
2. Read it again to sum d = Σ e<sup>x<sub>j</sub> - m</sup>.
3. Read it a third time and write y<sub>i</sub> = e<sup>x<sub>i</sub> - m</sup> / d.

That is three reads and one write per element. The naive version without the
max is two reads and one write. Maxim Milakov and Natalia Gimelshein count
it the same way in *Online normalizer calculation for softmax* (2018): four
memory accesses per element for the safe softmax against three for the naive
one. A softmax does a handful of operations per element, so like the kernels
in chapter 12 it is memory-bound (chapter 19). The extra pass costs time
directly.

### The online softmax

The paper's fix is to find the max and the sum in the same pass. Keep a
running max m and a running sum d of e<sup>x<sub>j</sub> - m</sup> over the
elements seen so far. When the next element x arrives:

- m<sub>new</sub> = max(m, x)
- d<sub>new</sub> = d · e<sup>m - m<sub>new</sub></sup> + e<sup>x - m<sub>new</sub></sup>

The second line is the whole idea. d was a sum of terms shifted by the old
max. Multiplying it by e<sup>m - m<sub>new</sub></sup> shifts every one of
those terms to the new max at once, without reading them again. If the max did
not change, the factor is e<sup>0</sup> = 1. If it grew, the factor is less
than 1 and the old terms shrink, exactly as they would have if we had known
the true max from the start. After the last element, m is the max of the row
and d is the safe softmax's sum. One more pass writes the output.

That is two reads and one write per element: the naive softmax's traffic with
the safe softmax's stability. The paper measured up to 1.3x faster softmax on
a Tesla V100 for vectors of about 4000 elements in large batches, close to the 4 / 3 ratio of
memory accesses. That figure is theirs; the chapter's kernels let you measure
your own.

### Merging two partial results

The update above takes one element at a time. The paper also writes it as an
operation on pairs. Let a chunk of the row be summarized by [m, d]: its max,
and the sum of e<sup>x - m</sup> over it. Two chunks combine as

[m<sub>1</sub>, d<sub>1</sub>] ⊕ [m<sub>2</sub>, d<sub>2</sub>] = [m, d<sub>1</sub> e<sup>m<sub>1</sub> - m</sup> + d<sub>2</sub> e<sup>m<sub>2</sub> - m</sup>], with m = max(m<sub>1</sub>, m<sub>2</sub>).

Each sum is rescaled to the larger max, then they are added. The paper shows
that ⊕ is associative and commutative, which is what a parallel reduction
needs (chapter 13): threads can summarize their own elements, then merge the
pairs in a tree, in any grouping. One element x is the pair [x, 1].

FlashAttention (chapter 25) is built on this merge. It computes softmax over
a row of attention scores that is never stored whole: each tile of scores
gives a partial [m, d], and the partial output computed with the old max is
rescaled by the same e<sup>m<sub>old</sub> - m<sub>new</sub></sup> factor when
a new tile raises the max.

  not the result or the number of reads: online always reads each element
  twice, safe three times.

The sim computes in double precision and models only the range of each
format: values above its largest finite number become inf, values below its
smallest become 0. It does not model rounding, caches, or the threads inside
a chunk.

## Mapping rows to threads

A softmax over a `[rows, cols]` matrix is independent per row, and each row
is one reduction (for the max and sum) followed by a map (the normalize). The
usual mapping is **one block per row**. The block's threads stride over the
row with `i += blockDim.x`, so consecutive threads read consecutive elements
and every load is coalesced (chapter 6). Each thread keeps its own [m, d]
pair. Then the block merges the 256 pairs with the reduction from chapter 13:
five `__shfl_xor_sync` steps inside each warp, one pair per warp through
shared memory, and five more steps. With `xor` instead of `down`, every lane
ends with the result, so no broadcast is needed afterwards. The merge only
replaces `+` with ⊕, so the shuffles carry two floats instead of one.

For short rows, one block is too much. A row of 64 floats on a 256-thread
block leaves 192 threads with nothing to load. **One warp per row** fits
better: the 32 lanes cover the row, the merge is five shuffles, and there is
no shared memory and no `__syncthreads()` at all. `softmax_online_warp` puts
four rows in each block this way. Very long rows go the other way: several
blocks per row, each producing an [m, d] pair that a second step merges.

### What fusion buys

Count the traffic in PyTorch-style eager code, where every operation is its
own kernel (chapter 12). The Triton softmax tutorial does this for
`x - x.max(dim=1)`, `exp`, `sum` and the divide, and finds that it reads
5MN + 2M elements and writes 3MN + 2M for an M x N matrix. One fused kernel
that reads the row, keeps it on chip and writes the result moves MN of each.
The tutorial's Triton kernel does exactly that: one program per row, the
whole row loaded into registers as one block, max, `exp`, sum and divide on
chip, one store. On a row that fits, it reads each element once.

Per float32 element, the kernels in this chapter move:

| Kernel | Reads | Writes | Bytes |
|---|---|---|---|
| `softmax_safe`, three passes | 3 | 1 | 16 |
| `softmax_online`, two passes | 2 | 1 | 12 |
| Triton `softmax_kernel`, row on chip | 1 | 1 | 8 |

For a 4096 x 1024 matrix those are {mib(4)}, {mib(3)} and {mib(2)} MiB of
traffic. At the {a100.name}'s {a100.memoryGbPerS} GB/s that would take
{us(4)}, {us(3)} and {us(2)} µs if every access went to DRAM: a model
estimate, not a measurement. In practice a 4 KB row read three times in a row
by the same block is likely to hit in L1 or L2 (chapter 9), so the second and
third reads may never reach DRAM. Measure the difference rather than assume
it. Keeping the row in registers stops working when it does not fit; then the
online two-pass kernel is the best a single launch can do.

## LayerNorm

LayerNorm (Ba, Kiros and Hinton, 2016) normalizes each row of H values to
mean 0 and variance 1, then applies a learned scale γ and shift β per column:

y<sub>i</sub> = (x<sub>i</sub> - μ) / √(σ<sup>2</sup> + ε) · γ<sub>i</sub> + β<sub>i</sub>

with μ the mean of the row and σ<sup>2</sup> = (1/H) Σ (x<sub>j</sub> - μ)<sup>2</sup>,
the population variance (PyTorch uses this biased estimator too). ε, by
default 10<sup>-5</sup> in `torch.nn.LayerNorm`, keeps the division finite
when a row is constant and σ<sup>2</sup> is 0.

It has the same shape as softmax: a reduction over the row, then a map. And
it has its own numerical trap.

### Variance in one pass, the wrong way and the right way

The two-pass variance reads the row once for μ and once more for the squared
deviations. The tempting one-pass formula, σ<sup>2</sup> = E[x<sup>2</sup>] -
E[x]<sup>2</sup>, accumulates Σx and Σx<sup>2</sup> together. It subtracts
two large, nearly equal numbers: for values near 1000 with a spread of 1,
E[x<sup>2</sup>] is about 10<sup>6</sup>, and float32 keeps about 7 decimal
digits, so most of the variance's digits are gone before the subtraction. The
test `test_naive_variance_cancels` shows an error of more than 1% on such a
row.

**Welford's algorithm** (1962) updates a count n, the running mean and M2,
the running sum of squared deviations from the mean, one value at a time:

- n ← n + 1
- δ = x - mean
- mean ← mean + δ / n
- M2 ← M2 + δ · (x - mean)

with σ<sup>2</sup> = M2 / n at the end. It only ever adds deviations from
the current mean, which are small, so it does not cancel.

For a GPU, each thread runs Welford over its own elements, and the block
merges the partial results. Chan, Golub and LeVeque (1979) give the merge of
two sets A and B:

- n = n<sub>A</sub> + n<sub>B</sub>
- δ = mean<sub>B</sub> - mean<sub>A</sub>
- mean = mean<sub>A</sub> + δ · n<sub>B</sub> / n
- M2 = M2<sub>A</sub> + M2<sub>B</sub> + δ<sup>2</sup> · n<sub>A</sub> n<sub>B</sub> / n

It is the same pattern as the softmax's ⊕: a small summary per chunk, and a
merge that fixes up one summary when the other's reference point differs.
`layernorm_welford` shuffles the three floats through the same warp and
block reduction as the softmax.

### Why one kernel

Written as separate eager operations, LayerNorm is a chain: a mean, a
subtract, a square, another mean, an add of ε and a reciprocal square root,
a multiply, then a multiply by γ and an add of β. Each step reads and writes
a full-size array, the pattern chapter 12 removes. Fused, the kernel reads
the row for the statistics, reads it again to normalize, scales and shifts in
registers, and writes once: two reads and a write, or one read and a write
when the row stays on chip as in the Triton version. The kernel also writes
μ and 1/√(σ<sup>2</sup> + ε) for each row, two floats, because the backward
pass needs them.

The Triton kernel uses the two-pass formula instead of Welford: with the row
already in registers, the second pass over it costs no memory traffic, and
the two-pass variance is accurate.

The CUDA kernels are in a header with no host code, so the tests can compile
them with NVRTC on a machine without `nvcc`. `cuda/softmax.cu` runs each one
on a matrix whose row 0 holds a 1000 and whose row 1 has a mean of 1000,
checks it against a CPU reference in double precision and times it. The
Numba kernels reduce through a shared memory tree instead of shuffles, so the
tests can run them on Numba's simulator, which has no shuffles. All kernels
use `expf`, the accurate library function; CUDA also has `__expf`, a faster
intrinsic with lower accuracy (CUDA Programming Guide, Mathematical Functions
appendix). The Triton tutorial notes that `tl.exp` is fast but approximate,
like `__expf`.

## Measure it yourself

On a GPU, from `kernels/ch24-softmax`:

```sh
make -C cuda run
python numba/softmax.py
python triton/softmax.py
ncu --kernel-name regex:softmax_ --metrics dram__bytes_read.sum,dram__bytes_write.sum,gpu__time_duration.sum ./cuda/softmax
```

Each program prints the mean time per launch and an effective bandwidth that
counts the matrix read once and written once. Things to look for:

- `dram__bytes_read.sum` for `softmax_safe` against `softmax_online`. If the
  rows stay in cache between passes, both read about the matrix size from
  DRAM and their times are close. Make the rows longer, `./cuda/softmax 512 65536`,
  and see whether the gap opens.
- `softmax_online_warp` against `softmax_online` for short rows, such as
  `./cuda/softmax 65536 64`, where most threads of a 256-thread block have no
  element.
- How close the effective bandwidth comes to your GPU's memory bandwidth.

## Exercises

**1. Merge by hand.** Two chunks of a row are summarized by [2, 1.5] and
[5, 1.2]. What is the merged pair?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The larger max is 5. The first sum is rescaled by e<sup>2 - 5</sup> =
e<sup>-3</sup> ≈ 0.0498, the second by e<sup>0</sup> = 1. The pair is
[{ex1.m}, 1.5 · 0.0498 + 1.2] = [{ex1.m}, {fmt(ex1.d)}].

</details>

**2. Where does FP16 break?** What is the largest score for which `exp`
stays finite in FP16, and in FP32? Which presets in the simulation make the
naive method fail in each?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

ln(65504) = {lim16} for FP16 and ln(3.4 x 10<sup>38</sup>) = {lim32} for
FP32. The increasing row reaches 12, which fails only in FP16 (its last two
values, 11.2 and 12, overflow). The row with a 100 fails in both. The random
row stays below 4 and works in both.

</details>

**3. Merge two Welford results.** Thread A has seen 1, 2 and 3; thread B
has seen 4 and 5. Give each thread's (n, mean, M2) and merge them.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A: n = {wa.n}, mean = {wa.mean}, M2 = (1 - 2)<sup>2</sup> + 0 + (3 - 2)<sup>2</sup> = {wa.m2}.
B: n = {wb.n}, mean = {wb.mean}, M2 = 0.25 + 0.25 = {wb.m2}.
δ = 4.5 - 2 = 2.5, n = 5, mean = 2 + 2.5 · 2 / 5 = {wab.mean},
M2 = 2 + 0.5 + 6.25 · 3 · 2 / 5 = {wab.m2}. The variance is {wab.m2} / 5 =
{wab.m2 / wab.n}, the population variance of 1 to 5.

</details>

**4. Fix the bug.** Someone writes the online update as
`d = d + expf(x - m_new)` and updates `m` as before. On which inputs is the
result still right, and what goes wrong otherwise?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

It is right only when the max never changes after the first element, that
is when x[0] is already the row's max. Otherwise the terms added before the
max grew are still scaled to the old, smaller max, so they are too large by a
factor e<sup>m<sub>new</sub> - m<sub>old</sub></sup> each time. The sum comes
out too big and the probabilities too small. On the increasing row every
chunk raises the max, so the error is largest there.

</details>

**5. Pick the mapping.** A model applies softmax to a 65536 x 64 matrix of
attention scores. How many threads of a 256-thread block have an element to
load with one block per row, and what would you use instead?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

64 of 256: the other 192 threads, three quarters of the block, take part in
the reduction but load nothing. One warp per row gives each lane two
elements, needs no shared memory and no barrier, and puts four rows in each
256-thread block. That is `softmax_online_warp`.

</details>
