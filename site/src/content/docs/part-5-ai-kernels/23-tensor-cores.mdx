The faster number comes from a different unit that does a different
operation: a small matrix multiply-accumulate per instruction, issued by a
whole warp, on inputs with fewer bits. The price is precision. This chapter
shows what those bits are, why tensor cores add in FP32 even when they
multiply in FP16, and how a warp shares one matrix multiply between its 32
lanes.

## A floating-point number, bit by bit

Every format here stores a sign bit `s`, an exponent field `e` of E bits and
a mantissa field `m` of M bits. For a normal number, the value is

```text
(-1)^s x 1.m x 2^(e - bias)        1.m is binary: 1 + m / 2^M
```

The bias is 2<sup>E-1</sup> - 1, so the exponent field stores both positive
and negative powers of two. Two field values are special. `e = 0` means a
**subnormal** number, `0.m x 2^(1 - bias)`, which fills the gap between the
smallest normal number and zero with evenly spaced values; with `m = 0` too
it is zero. All ones in `e` means infinity (`m = 0`) or NaN.

The exponent bits set the **range**, the mantissa bits the **precision**.
Read the table in pairs:

- **FP16 and BF16** both take 16 bits. FP16 (IEEE 754 binary16) spends 5 on
  the exponent and 10 on the mantissa: about 3 decimal digits, but nothing
  above 65,504. BF16 keeps FP32's 8 exponent bits and FP32's range, and keeps
  only 7 mantissa bits. BF16 is the top half of an FP32 number, so converting
  is rounding off the low 16 bits.
- **TF32** has FP32's exponent and FP16's mantissa, 19 bits in all. It is not
  a storage format: values sit in ordinary 32-bit FP32 registers and memory,
  and the tensor cores use only the top 19 bits.
- **FP8** comes in two encodings, both 8 bits. E4M3 has more precision, E5M2
  more range. The FP8 paper by Micikevicius et al. recommends "E4M3 for
  weight and activation tensors, and E5M2 for gradient tensors". E5M2
  follows the IEEE rules for infinity and NaN and is FP16 with 8 fewer
  mantissa bits. E4M3 has no infinity and only one NaN pattern per sign,
  `S.1111.111`, so its top exponent holds ordinary numbers up to 448 instead
  of the 240 that IEEE rules would allow.

### Rounding

A real number almost never falls exactly on a value the format has, so the
hardware rounds it, by default to the nearest one, and on a tie to the one
whose last mantissa bit is 0 ("round to nearest, ties to even"). Between
2<sup>k</sup> and 2<sup>k+1</sup> neighbouring values are
2<sup>k-M</sup> apart, so rounding moves a normal number by at most half of
that: a relative error of at most 2<sup>-(M+1)</sup>, the last column of the
table. FP16 0.1 is {hex(f16, tenth)} = {fmtValue(tenth.value)}, off by
{tenth.relError.toExponential(1)}. BF16 1/3 is {hex(bf16, third)} =
{fmtValue(third.value)}, off by {third.relError.toExponential(1)}.

Three things can go worse than that:

- **Overflow.** A value past the largest number becomes infinity. In FP16
  that happens at 65,520, not far above values a sum of squares can reach.
- **Underflow.** Below the smallest normal number, the spacing stays fixed
  while the numbers shrink, so subnormals have fewer significant bits. At or
  below half the smallest subnormal, the value becomes 0.
- **NaN.** E4M3 has no infinity. A conversion that does not saturate turns
  an overflow into NaN; CUDA's `__nv_cvt_float_to_fp8` takes a
  `__NV_SATFINITE` flag that clamps to 448 instead.

- **Set every exponent bit of BF16.** You get infinity, or NaN once any

  last mantissa bit cleared is 448.

## Why the accumulator is FP32

A matrix multiply is a set of dot products, and a dot product is a long
sum. Each add rounds. With an FP16 running sum, every partial sum keeps 11
significant bits, and the errors pile up with the length K of the sum.
Worse, once the sum is large, small terms vanish into the rounding. The
Numba kernel in this chapter adds 4,096 ones and gets 2,048 with an FP16
accumulator: at 2,048 the spacing of FP16 numbers is 2, 2,048 + 1 is a tie,
and the tie goes back to 2,048, every time. With an FP32 accumulator the
answer is 4,096.

Tensor cores keep the inputs small and the sum wide. The product of two FP16
numbers has at most 22 significant bits, so it fits exactly in FP32's 24,
and "FP16 inputs, FP32 accumulate" costs little accuracy beyond
rounding the inputs. That is the setting the peak rates below assume, and on
the {t4.name}, the {a100.name} and the {h100.name} NVIDIA lists the same
tensor rate for an FP32 accumulator as for an FP16 one. An FP16 accumulator
saves registers and output bytes, not tensor-core time.

Training a network in FP16 needs two more measures, from Micikevicius et al.,
*Mixed Precision Training*: an FP32 master copy of the weights that the
updates are added to, and **loss scaling**. Some gradients are smaller than
2<sup>-24</sup>, the smallest FP16 subnormal, and would become 0.
Multiplying the loss by a factor S, often a power of two, scales every
gradient by S by the chain rule and lifts them into FP16's range; the
optimizer divides by S in FP32 before the update. Dynamic loss scaling, as
in PyTorch's `torch.amp.GradScaler`, lowers S when a gradient overflows and
skips that step. BF16 has FP32's range, so BF16 training usually needs no
loss scaling. FP8 uses the same idea per tensor: the FP8 paper chooses a
scale for each tensor so that its largest value lands near the largest FP8
number.

## What a tensor core computes

A tensor core instruction computes

```text
D = A x B + C
```

for small fixed tiles: on the shape used below, A is 16 x 16, B is 16 x 8,
and C and D are 16 x 8. The instruction is issued by a whole warp at once.
No single lane holds A or B: each of the 32 lanes holds a few elements of
each matrix in its registers, called its **fragment**, and the tensor core
gathers them across the warp. All 32 lanes must take part, so a tensor-core
instruction inside a branch that splits a warp is undefined (the
CUDA programming guide warns that it is "likely to hang").

The formats a tensor core accepts depend on the GPU, from NVIDIA's PTX ISA
target notes for `mma` and the CUDA programming guide's WMMA tables:

| | {t4.name} ({t4.architecture}, sm_75) | {a100.name} ({a100.architecture}, sm_80) | {h100.name} ({h100.architecture}, sm_90) |
|---|---|---|---|
| FP16 in, FP16 or FP32 accumulate | yes | yes | yes |
| INT8 in, INT32 accumulate | yes | yes | yes |
| BF16 in, FP32 accumulate | no | yes | yes |
| TF32 in, FP32 accumulate | no | yes | yes |
| FP64 | no | yes | yes |
| FP8 E4M3, E5M2 in | no | no | yes |

The PTX ISA lists BF16 and TF32 `mma` as needing sm_80 and FP8 as needing
sm_89 or higher; NVIDIA's Hopper architecture article lists FP8 among the
H100's tensor-core data types.

then to FP8.

## Who holds what

The CUDA C++ API for tensor cores is WMMA (warp matrix multiply-accumulate),
in `<mma.h>`. Its fragments are opaque: the programming guide says "the
mapping of matrix elements into fragment internal storage is unspecified and
subject to change in future architectures." One level down, the PTX
instruction `mma.sync` does document its layout, and that is the
instruction Triton and libraries such as CUTLASS emit on Ampere. For
`mma.sync.aligned.m16n8k16.row.col.f32.f16.f16.f32` (FP16 A and B, FP32 C
and D), the PTX ISA gives, with `groupID = laneid >> 2` and
`threadID_in_group = laneid % 4`:

```text
A, 16 x 16, a0..a7 in four .f16x2 registers
  row = groupID           for a0, a1, a4, a5;   groupID + 8 for a2, a3, a6, a7
  col = 2 * threadID_in_group + (i & 1)   for i < 4;   the same + 8 for i >= 4
B, 16 x 8, b0..b3 in two .f16x2 registers
  row = 2 * threadID_in_group + (i & 1)   for i < 2;   the same + 8 for i >= 2
  col = groupID
C and D, 16 x 8, c0..c3 in four .f32 registers
  row = groupID for i < 2;   groupID + 8 for i >= 2
  col = 2 * threadID_in_group + (i & 1)
```

Pick a lane and see its 8 + 4 + 4 elements, or click any cell to see which
lane holds it and in which register. Steps 4 to 7 follow the four outputs
of the selected lane: each needs a row of A held by 4 lanes and a column of
B held by 4 other lanes.

The PTX ISA lists this f16 shape as needing sm_80. The {t4.name} (sm_75)
has `mma.sync` m16n8k8 for FP16, and WMMA's 16 x 16 x 16 works on it.
Hopper adds `wgmma`, issued by a warpgroup of four warps, which reads B (and
optionally A) from shared memory, with tiles up to 64 x 256 x 16; it is
beyond this chapter.

## The code

The CUDA version uses WMMA. Each warp declares three fragments, loads a
16 x 16 tile of A and of B with `load_matrix_sync`, multiplies them into
the accumulator with `mma_sync`, steps along K, and writes the 16 x 16
result with `store_matrix_sync`. The last argument of each load is the
leading dimension: the number of elements between consecutive rows, which
the guide requires to be a multiple of 8 for `__half`. The tiles come
straight from global memory. A fast GEMM stages them through shared memory
as in chapter 20, which is what cuBLAS and CUTLASS do.

numba-cuda 0.30.4 has no WMMA and no `mma`, so the Numba tab cannot use the
tensor cores. It shows the arithmetic instead: FP16 arrays (NumPy
`float16`), converted to FP32 for an FP32 sum in one kernel, and summed in
FP16 with `cuda.fp16.hfma` in the other. These run on the CUDA cores.

The tests in `test_correctness.py` compare every kernel against an FP64
NumPy product of the same FP16 inputs, with error bounds set by the
accumulator's precision: about K x 2<sup>-24</sup> of the sum of
|a| x |b| for FP32, K x 2<sup>-11</sup> for FP16. They include the
2,048 stall above. Without a GPU, they run the Numba kernels on the
simulator and the Triton kernel in its interpreter, compile the WMMA header
with NVRTC for sm_75, sm_80 and sm_90, and compile the Numba kernels to PTX.

## On the roofline

Chapter 19 put the FP16 tensor ridge of the {a100.name} at
{ridge16.toFixed(0)} FLOP/byte, against 9.6 for FP32. A square GEMM that
reads A and B once in FP16 and writes C once in FP32 moves 8N<sup>2</sup>
bytes for 2N<sup>3</sup> FLOPs: N / 4 FLOP/byte. It needs N above
{(4 * ridge16).toFixed(0)} before it can be compute-bound on the tensor
cores. Every tile size, register and shared-memory decision from chapter
20 matters more here, because the faster the arithmetic, the more reuse each
byte needs to keep up.

## Measure it

On a GPU, from `kernels/ch23-tensor-cores`:

```sh
make -C cuda run ARCH=80
python numba/fp16_gemm.py
python triton/matmul.py
ncu --section ComputeWorkloadAnalysis --section InstructionStats ./cuda/wmma_gemm
```

The CUDA program prints the time, the TFLOPS and the largest error of each
WMMA kernel; the Triton script does the same for FP16, BF16, TF32 and FP32
inputs. Things to look for:

- The error of the FP16 accumulator against the FP32 one, at the same speed
  or close to it.
- In the Triton output, the TF32 error against the `"ieee"` error, and the
  time between them.
- In Nsight Compute, HMMA instructions in the instruction statistics and
  the tensor pipe's utilization in the compute workload section. No HMMA
  means the work did not reach the tensor cores.

No GPU was available for this chapter. Every program here was compiled
(CUDA with nvcc and NVRTC for sm_75, sm_80 and sm_90, Triton to PTX) and
tested on the CPU, but none was timed, so no times or TFLOPS are quoted.

## Exercises

**1. Read the bits.** What is FP16 `0 10001 1010000000`?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Exponent field 10001 = 17, minus the bias 15: 2<sup>2</sup>. Mantissa
1.101 in binary = 1.625. The value is 1.625 x 4 = 6.5.

</details>

**2. Counting integers.** What is the largest N such that every integer from
0 to N is exact in BF16? In FP16?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A format with M mantissa bits has M + 1 significant bits, so every integer
up to 2<sup>M+1</sup> is exact and 2<sup>M+1</sup> + 1 is not. BF16:
2<sup>8</sup> = 256; 257 rounds to 256. FP16: 2<sup>11</sup> = 2,048, the
number the FP16 sum of ones stops at.

</details>

**3. Find the lane.** In `mma.m16n8k16`, which lane holds A(10, 5), and
under which name? Which lane holds D(3, 6)?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

A: row 10 is groupID + 8, so groupID = 2; column 5 is
2 x threadID_in_group + 1 with i &lt; 4, so threadID_in_group = 2. Lane =
4 x 2 + 2 = 10. Row + 8 and odd column, i &lt; 4: a3, the high half of
register 1. D: row 3 = groupID, column 6 = 2 x 3 + 0, so lane 4 x 3 + 3 =
15, element c0. Check both in the simulation.

</details>

**4. Loss scale.** A gradient is 2<sup>-27</sup>. What does it become in
FP16? What is the smallest power-of-two loss scale that keeps it nonzero?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The smallest FP16 subnormal is 2<sup>-24</sup>, and anything at or below
half of it, 2<sup>-25</sup>, rounds to 0. So 2<sup>-27</sup> becomes 0.
Scaled by 4 it is 2<sup>-25</sup>, a tie that goes to 0. Scaled by 8 it is
2<sup>-24</sup> exactly. Real loss scales are much larger, often
2<sup>10</sup> or more, to keep a few significant bits too.

</details>

**5. FP16 accumulate on tensor cores.** The WMMA kernel with an FP16
accumulator adds 16 products per `mma_sync` before it rounds into C. Would
it stall at 2,048 on the all-ones test like the Numba kernel?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Not there. Each step adds 16, and from 2,048 to 4,096 FP16 values are 2
apart, so 2,048 + 16 is exact, and so is every sum up to 4,096. A sum of
ones would first lose steps of 16 above 2<sup>15</sup> = 32,768, where the
spacing becomes 32 and 32,768 + 16 is a tie. How the tensor core rounds
inside one instruction is not specified for WMMA, so this argument only
covers the rounding into C.

</details>
